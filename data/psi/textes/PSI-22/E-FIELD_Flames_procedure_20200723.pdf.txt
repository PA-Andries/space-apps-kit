1. Introduction 
The narrative description of the experimental procedure is from the ‘Methods / Experimental’ section of 
the following conference paper: 
Y.-C. Chien, J. Tinajero, D. Stocker, U. Hegde, and D. Dunn -Rankin, “Electric Field Effects on Flames in 
Microgravity on the International Space Station,” 2018 Spring Technical Meeting of the Central States 
Section of The Combustion Institute, Minneapolis, MN, 20-22 May 2018. 
This description also appears in Section 4.B Overview of Experiment Test Operations of the E-FIELD Flames 
Final Report.  Note that the referenced Figure 4 (showing the coflow burner geometry) is not included in 
this document. 
 
The CIR experiments are configured, but not conducted, by the ISS crew.  Thus far, ACME’s activity has 
included the majority of the crew members, regardle ss of their nationalities.  Most often the astronauts 
and cosmonauts exchange gas bottles, mass flow controllers, and igniter tips.  In the latter case, the igniter 
tip exchange can occur after failure or proactively before failure when the crew already ha s changes to 
make changes in th e interior of the insert.  While these activities may seem mundane, the crew play a 
critical role in setting up the hardware for each experiment.  As an extreme example, there were crew 
activities on 8 separate days in Sept.-Oct. 2017 to configure the CIR and ACME hardware for the first ACME 
experiment, namely the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment.  In contrast, only two 
crew days were required to reconfigure the hardware for the first E -FIELD Flames tests because of the 
initial use of the same coflow burner.  Planning is critical because crew activity is scheduled three weeks 
in advance, for which detailed procedures must be prepared and uplinked to the ISS. 
 
All CIR tests are remotely commanded from the ground from NASA’s Glenn ISS Payload Operations Center 
(GIPOC) in Cleveland, Ohio.  Typically , four to six people staff four consoles during the testing.  The CIR 
hardware is controlled from the Rack Officer (RO) and Data Management Officer (DMO) consoles, and the 
ACME hardware is control led from the Payload Developer (PD) console.  Two shifts of personnel are 
scheduled to staff these three consoles on test days because they typically last 14 hours with the many 
pre- and post-testing activities.  Given that the tests are remotely commanded, the operations are carried 
out in the day and evening in Cleveland, e.g., from 7 AM to 9 PM.  A day’s tests, i.e., with flames, are 
normally conducted in a period of 10 hours of less.  Tests are normally conducted on three weekdays per 
two weeks.  On intervening weekdays, the image data is downlinked and the crew often swap one or both 
gas bottles.  Transferring and downlinking data from a day’s tests also requires a 2 -shift day for the RO 
and DMO consoles, where it is alway s necessary to verify that data  was downlinked without errors and 
that data is not missing.  Those two positions are also staffed to guide and oversee the crew activity.  As 
a result, the CIR consoles are normally actively staffed on four weekdays per week.  A team of four carry 
out the detailed planning for the CIR and ACME console staff, and the daily operational plans typically 
exceed 50 pages on days including testing. 
 
The science console allows for review of the analog video and non-imaging telemetry (at 1 Hz) in near real 
time but doesn’t allow for commanding.  In the case of E-FIELD Flames, arrangements were made so that 
the analog video is streamed to a secure computer at UC Irvine to allow the research team to participate 
in test operations and to recommend adjustments to the day’s planned parameters.  That team does not 
see the telemetry during the testing, but is in communication via phone and e -mail with their NASA 
research partners at the GRC ISS Payload Operations Center (GIPOC).  While there are many preparatory 
steps, the tests are ultimately conducted using an uploaded software script and parameter files.  In 
advance of the test day, the UC Irvine science team plans the nearly 60 parameters in the voltage sweep 
script and specifies other para meters that are configured outs ide of the script, such as camera settings.

It is also possible to effectively conduct tests in a ‘manual’ mode, where parameters are changed in 
response to the analog video downlink or telemetry.  For example, the electric field potential can be 
adjusted to approach a stability or soot limit. 
 
Typically, ten E-FIELD Flames tests are conducted per test day. Despite the short test durations, the low 
rate results from several unavoidable factors.  After a test is conducted, the 125 -Hz non-imaging data is 
downlinked for immediate review by the scien ce team.  A small sample of images are often downlinked 
to ensure that the camera exposures are appropriate, where the times for those images are typically 
selected based on non-imaging data to provide extreme examples.  The review helps to verify parameters 
or plan for adjustments in subsequent tests, e.g., to select the voltage range after determining where the 
ion current saturates for the given flame conditions.  After a test is conducted, a fan circulates the chamber 
atmosphere after which the chamber is vented (to space) back to atmospheric pressure, if needed.  
Midway through a day’s E-FIELD Flames tests, the atmosphere is circulated for 15 minutes through a filter 
cartridge filled with molecular sieves to capture any soot and adsorb at least a portion of the combustion 
products, e.g., carbon dioxide, water vapor, and incomplete combustion products.  The tests are only 
conducted when both Ku and S band communication is available through NASA’s Tracking and Data Relay 
Satellite (TDRS) System.  Depending on the various orbits, there are periodic communication outages with 
a loss of signal (LOS) that can be a short handover (from one satellite to another) or can last as long as 30 
minutes. 
 
A variety of constraints limit the number of tests that can be performed, both overall and on a daily basis.  
The next combustion experiments in the CIR will feature solid fuels and focus on flame spread and material 
flammability.  That follow-on plan limits the duration of ACME operations, where each ACME experiment 
is allotted four months for testing.  Although it requires additional crew time, these allotments are split 
into two rounds so that the investigators have a break from testing for careful review of the early results 
so that they can maximize the scientific outcome of their second round.  This paper reports on early results 
from the first round of E-FIELD Flames. 
 
While nitrogen is available from the space station, the combustion testing is limited by the available gas 
bottles that were and are launched to supp ort the tests.  For E -FIELD Flames and most of ACME’s 
experiments, the test matrices used to plan those gas resources were established in 2010.  At that time, 
NASA limited the scope of the ACME tests matrices with t he intent of focused experiments with lim ited 
objectives rather than comprehensive experiments.  As such, the general target was 50 tests per 
experiment.  For most ACME experiments, the limiting gas constraint is the oxygen/nitrogen bottles used 
to fill the chamber.  This resource constraint is exacerbated by the CIR because it is equipped with a pair 
of redundant safety timers to limit the fuel that can flow into the combustion chamber (where the timers 
can be set for the oxidizer flow in the case of inver se flame testing).  The timers can only b e reset by 
venting the combustion chamber.  Therefore, the timer limit combined with other constraints effectively 
corresponds to the day’s maximum flow time for the fuel (or more generally, the limiting reactant).  For 
E-FIELD Flames, the safety timers are set to the maximum value of 999 seconds, which corresponds to the 
10 tests typically completed per day. 
 
Another constraint is the available time in a day, where the console personnel and ISS resources, such as 
the video downlink, are planned for a limited number of hours.  When necessary for the research, NASA 
does have continuous 24-hour/day operations for some non-CIR experiments.  But as discussed, the ACME 
tests are typically two minutes or less in duration, s o the testing is almost always limited to  the planned 
durations.

Data limits are yet another constraint, where the on -orbit hardware can store a limited amount of data.  
More importantly, 127,000 MB is the most that can be reasonably managed in a two -shift transfer and 
downlink day.  Therefore, tests days are limited to this amount of data, where image data from select 
cameras is often archived at reduced rates to mitigate this challenge.  Nonetheless, the limit sometimes 
forces decisions to abandon recorded images. 
 
This detailed description of operations and hardware provides the context under which the ACME 
experiments are conducted.  Their complexity makes the flame conditions seem trivial in comparison.  It 
is a simple coflow flame, with just a small j et of fuel surrounded by a weak coflow of  ‘air’ which also fills 
the chamber, but where tests are also conducted with no coflow.  A test includes starting the gas flows to 
prime the system, energizing the igniter to initiate a flame, retracting the igniter, and allowing the flame 
to steady slight ly before energizing the electric field to its initial value.  The flame is again allowed to 
steady before beginning a voltage sweep over a specified range with steps or smooth ramps of specified 
voltage changes and durations.  The sweep can be in one direction or can include cycles of sweeps.  After 
each test, a duplicate field sweep is made to ensure that there are no current leaks, i.e., other than the 
ion current through the flame. 
 
The first phase tests in E -FIELD Flames employ the coflow burner geomet ry shown in Figure 4, with the 
fuel tube extending slightly above the burner surface.  The results shown in this paper are for tests 
conducted with 100% methane in a chamber atmosphere and coflow (when used) of synt hetic ‘air’ (i.e., 
21/79 oxygen/nitrogen by volume) at a nominal pressure of 100 kPa.  The mesh is held at a negative 
potential relative to the burner in these tests.  The sweeps shown in this paper extend to at least -7 kV, 
where the voltage is increased in small steps (e.g., of 100 V) lasting 1-2 seconds.  The details of the voltage 
changes can be seen in the resulting plots.

2. Test Sequences and Parameters All E-FIELD Flames tests conducted on the ISS 
were carried out using automated software w hich had its roots in  ACME’s Integrated Science 
Requirements Documents (ISRD, RDR version, Rev. B) and Science Operations Requirements 
Documents (SORD, Rev. A).  The E -FIELD Flames Science Requirements Document (SRD) is of course 
pertinent but only that wh ich was incorporated in the ISRD and SORD was used by the engineering 
team in developing the control software. 
 
Multiple software scripts and corresponding parameter files were prepared to address the various 
objectives explained in this excerpt from section 4.2 of the ACME ISRD.  The section is fully included with 
the important exception of the tables detailing the envisioned operational sequences.  Given that they 
were conceptual, they were left out to reduce the chance of a confused understanding of the engineering 
implementation.  For example, excessive electromagnetic interference (EMI) led to a change in the design 
of the electric field system limiting the voltage to a single polarity on a given test day.  As a result, the step 
response procedure could not be carried out as detailed in Table 4.2.2 of the ISRD.   
 
The E-FIELD experiment consists of 4 objectives corresponding to 4 series of tests  using 2 different 
diffusion flame burner configurations. The first three test series  provide the key fundamental 
information regarding the electrical character of diffusion flames and the influence of electric fields on 
soot formation; the final series focuses on electric field sensing and control of flames. 
 
General Operations 
Before any of the electric field experiments, the high voltage mesh should be installed (or moved into 
position). Leak current should be measured by sweeping the voltage  from 0 to 10kV and 0 to -10kV 
with no flame present. A steep rise in current under these conditions indicates corona discharge and 
sets the upper limit on the potential.  The test sequence involves a complete repeat voltage sweep 
with no flame to provide  a point -by-point current leak measurement. A similar sweep should be 
completed following flame extinguishment at the end of a test set. Only ion current would need to be 
measured during the no-flame sweep. In addition, excessive leak current at relatively low voltage may 
indicate electron emission from carbon buildup on the gas  jet flame tip. If the leak current exceed s 
10% of the flame ion current, cleaning of the  gas jet tip is requested. Cleaning consists of a manual 
wiping or possibly burning off the carbon with a modified flame condition. We expect this to be a rare 
request, but the continuous monitor of flameless ion current provides a reliable in-situ monitor. We 
assume that the chamber will be emptied and refilled approximately every 7-8 tests to minimize the 
oxygen concentration uncertainty in the test chamber. The exact times for settling and voltage steps 
may vary (they should be reconfigurable from the  ground), but a nominal expected operational 
sequence for one chamber fill and the key measurement data sets are shown below. 
 
(A) Voltage Sweep – collection of VI curves (or VCC curves) -- where the voltage is swept from 0-±5kV 
(or until flame blowout) and the ion current is monitored and  recorded. The sweep will consist of a 
series of discrete steps, where a settling time is allowed after each voltage change. At the same time, 
color and OH* video rec ords the fla me shape and luminosity (both broadband flame and soot 
luminosity and OH* chemiluminescence) throughout the sweep. A typical step sweep might comprise: 
15 second ignition & stabilization; 100 V/step, 0.3 sec settle between steps, and a 0.25 sec. ion current 
measurement at each point, to ±5kV or blowout. Blowout can be  detected by a sharp drop in ion 
current. Expected run time is 50 seconds per sweep (not including a background current sweep with 
the flame off). However, the time  between voltage steps for fl ame adjustment and data collection 
shall be adjustable via uplink between the values of 0-10 seconds.

(B) Step Response—This test will involve the rapid change of voltage from a base  level to a much 
higher (or lower) level, while the ion curre nt (and flame luminosity and shape) is monitored in time 
as it settles to its new steady value. The key measurable is the ion current as a function of time through 
the voltage step change and the flame’s subsequent settling. As in test set (A), video, luminosity, and 
chemiluminescence will record flame behaviors in response to an instantaneous change in the electric 
field. A typical experiment sequence might comprise a 15  second ignition and stabilization, step 
changes on the order of hundreds of volts, and  recording ion current for 2 seconds between step 
changes (where steps will not be limited to a single polarity). Settling time for these step changes in 
zero gravity (particularly, for soot related phenomena) may need to be adjusted via uplink; the test 
procedures shown are based on a nominal settling time observed in preliminary testing at 1g and the 
2.2 Second Drop Tower. The range of times between voltage steps may vary between 0-100 seconds. 
 
(C) Electric Field Effects on Soot – The following tests will demonstrate the effects of electric fields on 
soot (measured by broadband emission from the flame and soot volume fraction) by slowly increasing 
the coflow air velocity, comparing the flame  structure at each step to a flame affected purely by 
electric forcing. Conditions will be  uplinked and derived from experiments (A) and (B). Timing will 
depend on the burner, but each test will be allowed at least 100 seconds. Also, note that the step 
time between one coflow velocitity and the next shall be adjustable via uplink between the values of 
0-10 seconds. Broad solid angle collection luminosity, flame  images along with soot volume fraction 
and soot pyrometry will be collected. 
 
(D) Open Loop Sensing and Control – The experiments associated with control will be uplinked, based 
on the results of the above baseline mapping experiments (A) and  (B) above. Timing will depend on 
the condition, but each test will be allowed at least  70 seconds. The procedure is a series of voltage 
ramps (or small steps with  continuous data collection). Ion current and luminosity (broadband and 
CH*) fluctuations will be collected as well as flame images. The time between voltage  changes for 
flame adjustment and data collection shall be adjustable via uplink  between the values of 0 -10 
seconds. 
 
Although four types of E -FIELD Flames tests are discussed above, it was determined by 2017 that three 
types of tests could be used to address the four experimental objectives, where a separate script to 
investigate effects on the soot was not needed. 
 
While scripts were developed for the three remaining sequences, it was later determined that the open 
loop objectives could be accomplished by proper selection of parameter values with the voltage sweep 
script, e.g., limiting the voltage range.  Furthermore, the step response script was ultimately not employed 
because of the slow response time of the high -voltage power supply.  As such, all of the experiment’s 
flame tests were conducted with the voltage sweep script .  Therefore, that procedure is the only one to 
be further discussed in this document.

More specifically, the script was updated between testing on April 16 (GMT 18106) and April 23 (18113) 
with the addition of the parameter nitrogenPrimingDurationSec.  The parameter was added because it 
was observed that unwanted oscillations in the nitrogen flow often diminished with time.  Therefore, an 
early start of the nitrogen flow could reduce or eliminate oscillations while the flame was burning.  This 
feature can be seen in a simplified graphic of the test sequence (with constant flow rates) in Figure 2.  At 
the same time, four bracketing parameters were updated to enable the bracketed exposures to increase 
via multiplication by a specified constant rather than via addition of a constant  time.  The revised set of 
parameters was retained through the second round of testing with the gas-jet burner that was carried out 
in Sept. to Nov. 2018.

{

    // withFlame=false: Includes enable and initialization period. 
    // Disables at end. 
     
    if ( withFlame == false) 
    { 
        EFieldEnable.state = 1; 
        sendCommand(EFieldEnable); 
        print("E-field enabled"); 
 
        // include initialization period in measurement 
        print("Initializing E-Field to " + parameters.efieldInitializeVolts + " volts");

        sendCommand(EFieldSetVoltage);

        print("Waiting E-Field initialize time " + parameters.efieldInitializeTimeSec + " sec"); 
        if ( parameters.efieldInitializeTimeSec > 0 ) 
        { 
            var startTime = new Date().getTime(); 
            while ( updateDurationRemaining(startTime, parameters.efieldInitializeTimeSec) > 0 ) 
            { 
                mSleep(1000); 
            } 
        }

    if ( parameters.efieldRampRatePerSec != 0 ) 
    { 
        // perform a single ramp or a ramping staircase 
        EFieldRampedSignal.minVoltage = parameters.efieldMinVoltage; 
        EFieldRampedSignal.maxVoltage = parameters.efieldMaxVoltage; 
        EFieldRampedSignal.rampRatePerSec = parameters.efieldRampRatePerSec; 
        EFieldRampedSignal.rampDurationSecs = parameters.efieldRampDurationSecs; 
        EFieldRampedSignal.holdDurationSecs = parameters.efieldHoldDurationSecs; 
        EFieldRampedSignal.cycles = parameters.efieldCycles; 
        sendCommand(EFieldRampedSignal); 
        print("Ramping E-Field"); 
    } 
    else 
    { 
        // perform a single step or a staircase 
        EFieldSteppedSignal.minVoltage = parameters.efieldMinVoltage; 
        EFieldSteppedSignal.maxVoltage = parameters.efieldMaxVoltage; 
        EFieldSteppedSignal.stepVoltage = parameters.efieldStepVoltage; 
        EFieldSteppedSignal.stepDurationSecs = parameters.efieldStepDurationSecs; 
        EFieldSteppedSignal.cycles = parameters.efieldCycles; 
        sendCommand(EFieldSteppedSignal); 
        print("Stepping E-Field"); 
    } 
       
    // make sure waveform has started 
    mSleep(2000); 
       
    // wait for waveform to complete 
    while ( EFieldTelemetry.waveform != 0 ) 
    { 
        if ( withFlame ) 
        {

            mSleep(1000); 
        } 
    } 
       
    // Disable the E-Field 
    EFieldEnable.state = 0; 
    sendCommand(EFieldEnable); 
    print("Waveform complete. E-Field disabled");

    {

    sendCommand(StartIpsus);

4. Test Matrices 
For all ACME experiments, including E -FIELD Flames, the test planning is refined in multiple  steps as 
described in this section.  Despite refinement, the experiments are always expected to address the science 
objectives approved in the ACME ISRD. 
 
A. Test Matrices: Years in Advance 
The test matrices found in section 5 of the ACME Integrated Science Re quirements Document 
(ISRD, RDR version, Rev. B) were the basis for planning the equipment and resources, such as gas 
bottles, needed to carry out each of the NASA -selected ACME experiments.  For E-FIELD Flames, 
the test matrices are found in section 5.2.  The investigators were each directed to define matrices 
with required testing which included at most one burner configuration and no more than 
nominally 50 flames ignited.   Regarding these constraints specified by Project Scientist Dennis 
Stocker, the inte nt was to conduct focused rather than comprehensive research given the 
considerable resources required for testing on the International Space Station.    Meanwhile, there 
were no limits put on desired or highly desired tests because of NASA’s plan to only carry out tests 
designated as required. 
 
However, in the case of E-FIELD Flames, the science loss associated with the slow response time 
of the developed electric field system led to the decision to allow a second burner type and an 
increased number of required tests .  Specifically, the matrix was expanded to 74 flames rather 
than the planned 58, where it must be understood that voltage sweep tests as defined in the ISRD 
require 2 flames, where the voltage is increased in one test and decreased in the other.  As a result 
of this allowance, E-FIELD Flames was carried out with both the coflow burner (developed for the 
CLD Flame experiment) and a gas-jet burner with the same tube size as the coflow burner’s inner 
tube.  This enabled testing with and without the coflow and in the absence of the outer tube (i.e., 
with the gas-jet burner) in order to assess latter’s coupled effect on the electric field distribution 
and oxidizer entrainment. 
 
NASA Glenn’s ISS Control Board later decided to limit ACME testing to nominally 4 months of ISS 
testing per experiment which with preparatory operations, minor delays, repeat  testing due to 
science loss, etc. were expected to typically occur in a total of 5 months.  This decision was made 
with the expectation that the required testing, e.g., as specified in the ISRD, could at least mostly 
be accomplished in that time period assuming the availability of crew and other needed 
resources. 
 
As part of his planning, Stocker instigated an alternating sequence of experiment testing, wherein 
investigators would get a break in their testing in order to analyze early results in depth and with 
that knowledge achieve higher science outcomes in their second round of testing.  As a result, 
each experiment was expected to get 2 months of  testing per round, which meant nominally 8  
test days per round of testing.  Within that time constraint and available resources, experiments 
were ultimately permitted to plan for and conduct more tests than had been specified as required 
in the ISRD, despite the initial planning which ignored desired and highly desired testing. 
 
B. Test Matrices: Months in Advance 
At least a few months in advance of testing, ACME investigators are directed to submit a refined 
test matrix for the upcoming round  corresponding to available h ardware, gas bottles, and the 
agreed number of test days.  The refinement can be based on what is learned from experimental,

C. Test Matrices: Days in Advance 
The investigators are requir ed to submit their test parameters for all planned tests by noon 
Eastern a t least three business days in advance of each scheduled test day.  That submission 
includes all of test parameters within the script and additional ones which are outside of the script  
Most of the latter deal with camera settings which don’t change during a test, for example the 
filters used with the ACME Data Camera (DataCam) and the Cool Flames Investigation (CFI) 
intensified camera.  Outside of the scrip t, the investigators must again spec ify the chamber 
atmosphere(s) and when GCIP measurements, if any, are planned where those must correspond 
to the plan submitted months earlier .  The script parameters, e.g., specifications of flow rates, 
don’t need to co rrespond to those previously specified, but they must correspond to the 
hardware and gas bottles planned months earlier and generally the chamber atmospheres as well

D. Test Matrices: As-Run 
Within constraints, t he ACME tests conducted can use different parameter values than those 
specified in advance.  While the planning is critically important, the plans themselves are not 
particularly useful for interpreting the data .  This is because most tests were conducted with 
parameters that differed in at least a few ways from those planned days earlier. 
 
While it may seem obvious and simple enough to focus attention on the as-run parameter values 
(where an example E-FIELD Flames file name is 18073A3_VoltageSweep.txt), that is unfortunately 
not the case for ACME.  The record of the as -run script parameters was  initially reliant on the 
console staff to record the values.  Th at didn’t always occur as planned and the values were 
sometimes recreated from other records.  Given this shortcoming, the software was altered such 
that the as -run parameters values would au tomatically be saved.  However, it has been found 
that the software fails to save all parameter values.  The most assured way to get the as-run 
parameter values is presumably from the test day’s automated ACME log file, where an example 
name is 2018-10-17_12-26ACME.log.

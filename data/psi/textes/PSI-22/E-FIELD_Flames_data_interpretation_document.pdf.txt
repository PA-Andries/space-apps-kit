1. Introduction 
The purpose of this report is to  describe the data acquired for ACME’s E -FIELD Flames experiment  to 
facilitate its analysis.  The data is all digital , where the fuel was gaseous and the re was no collection of 
soot or the combustion gases.  For each test, the key data consists of image files and a file with the non-
imaging measurements.  Additional files such as camera metadata  and operational  logs provid e 
information that can support the analysis. 
 
It is recommended that readers interested in analyzing the E-FIELD Flames data begin by familiarizing 
themselves with the hardware  and instrumentation, either through the overview in the E-FIELD Flames 
Final Report or the more comprehensive  description found in the  ACME Engineering Implementation 
document. 
 
Those seeking to analyze the data should next review this document.  To interpret the non-imaging data, 
it is suggested that a sample data file be opened, named for example 18073A3_data.csv, which has 
comma-separated values and can be opened with spreadsheet software such as Microsoft Excel.  The data 
is in raw voltages, where conversion to engineering units requires study of ACME’s Software Interface 
Design Description, i.e., ACME-DOC-010 (where the latest version at the time of this writing is revision D).

2. E-FIELD Flames Overview 
This text is the “Overview” from the ACME/E-FIELD Flames Final Report, which provides descriptions of the 
science, preliminary results, and more.  It is suggested that it be reviewed by those interested in analyzing 
the E-FIELD Flames data. 
 
The Electric -Field Effects on Laminar D iffusion Flames ( E-FIELD Flames ) experiment is one of six 
independent experiments that make up the Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) 
project for which tests are conducted within the Combustion Integrated Rack ( CIR) on the Internati onal 
Space Station (ISS). 
 
The experiment’s Principal Investigator (PI) is Prof. Derek Dunn -Rankin of the University of California, 
Irvine ( UC Irvine).  Dr. Yu -Chien (Alice) Chien, also of UC Irvine, served as the Project Scientist at the 
university.  Meanwhile, Dennis P. Stocker of the NASA Glenn Research Center is NASA’s Project Scientist 
(PS) for ACME includ ing E-FIELD Flames and the rest of the AC ME investigations.  In preparatory drop 
testing, etc., Dr. Fumiaki Takahashi of the Case Western Reserve University (CWRU) served as the Deputy 
Project Scientist.  For ACME’s spaceflight operations, the NASA science team includes Drs. Uday G. Hegde 
and Vedha Nayagam who are also with CWRU. 
 
The experiment had two Co-Investigators, the first of which was Prof. Felix Weinberg of London’s Imperial 
College.  Prof. Weinberg was a pioneer in researching the effects of electrical fields on flames in 
microgravity, but unfortunately died in 2012 (see http://www.imperial.ac.uk/news/117808/tribute-felix-
weinberg/) before the hardware was built and the tests w ere conducted on the ISS.  The second Co -
Investigator was Dr. Sunny Karnani who did his doctoral research in preparation of the spaceflight 
experiment and afterward worked for ClearSign Combustion Corp., which marke ts Electrodynamic 
Combustion Control (https://www.clearsign.com/technology/electrodynamic-combustion-control/).  The 
corporation provided some support to the E -FIELD Flames research effort, but Karnani left Cl earSign to 
conduct research for the U.S. Army, effectively ending his intimate role in the project prior to the 
spaceflight operations.  He remains a collaborator. 
 
E-FIELD Flames operations were conducted in two rounds of testing during Increments 55-57, which were 
more specifically in March -May and September -November 2018.  The break in testing (from May to 
September) was intentional and planned years in advance to allow the research team to analyze early 
results and use the insights obtained from the ana lysis to plan the second round so as to maximize its 
scientific output.

3. Tests Conducted 
This text is an excerpt from the “Results” section of the ACME/E-FIELD Flames – 30-Day Operation 
Accomplishments Report for ISS Increments 57 and 58 (which is included in the ACME/E-FIELD Flames Final 
Report). 
  
A total of 219 flames were ignited for the E -FIELD Flames experiment, with 142 and 77 
respectively ignited during Increments 55-56 and 57-58.  Note that the investigation’s testing was 
completed during Increment 57, so no flames were ignited during Increment 58.  The first and 
second rounds of testing respectively occurred in March -May 2018 and September -November 
2018.  The two rounds of tests resulted in 120 and 99 ignited flames conducted with coflow and 
gas-jet burners, respectively.  The coflow burner was developed for the Coflow Laminar Diffusion 
Flame (CLD Flame) experiment, where CLD Flames’ second round occurred between the two 
rounds of the E-FIELD Flames experiment.  The ‘interruption’ in the E -FIELD Flames testing was 
intentionally planned years in advance to enable the PI team to review the initial results in detail 
and with that knowledge plan the second round of tests to maximize its scientific outcome. 
 
E-FIELD Flames does not have living subjects lik e a life science experiment, but the tests are 
independently conducted with two different gaseous fuels, methane and ethylene.  Both are 
simple hydrocarbon gases for which the chemistry i s well understood.  Methane (CH4) is the 
primary component of natural gas and tends to produce low levels of soot given the absence of 
carbon-carbon bonds.  Meanwhile, ethylene (C2H4), is highly sooting because of its carbon 
double bond.  For both fuels, t ests were conducted with pure fuel and fuel mixed with nitrogen 
over a broad range of dilutions.  For limited cases, the fuel and nitrogen was premixed on the 
ground prior to launch.  ACME bottles were launched with methane concentrations of 30%, 40%, 
45%, and 100% with a balance of nitrogen (on a volume basis).  Similarly, bottles were launched 
with ethylene concentrations of 30%, 50%, and 100%, again with a balance of nitrogen (on a 
volume basis).  But the ACME hardware also allows for simultaneous flow of fuel and nitrogen to 
the burner, allowing for on-orbit fuel dilution and testing at a continuum of fuel concentrations.  
In all cases, the coflow was 21% oxygen with a balance of nitrogen (on a volume basis).  
 
No samples (e.g., of soot) were collected f or E -FIELD Flames; all of the ISS results were 
electronically transmitted to the ground.  That is furthermore the case for all currently planned 
ACME experiments.

4. Data Interpretation 
This key section provides (1) a general description of the raw experimental data and associated files and 
(2) guidance on data conversions, e.g., to engineering units .  Only limited guidance is provided for the 
processing of image data, with the important exception of the conversion of the ACME DataCam images 
from the raw Halcon object (HOBJ) format. 
 
A. Non-Imaging Data 
Data and files discussed in this section include the following: 
Data Output File 
Mass Flow Controller (MFC) Setpoint File 
Fuel Oxidizer Management Assembly (FOMA) data 
Space Acceleration Measurement System (SAMS) data 
 
Some ACME experiments featured measurements made with the CIR’s Gas Chromatograph 
Instrumentation Package (GCIP), but it was not used for the E-FIELD Flames experiment and a such 
that data type is not addressed in this document. 
 
1. Data Output File 
The basic ACME non-imaging measurements for each test are together in a file described in 
Section 8.1 Data Output File on page 124 of Software Interface Design Description ( ACME-
DOC-010 Rev D).  The test number is made up of the year (2-digts), day of year (3 digits), and 
test (a letter and single-digit number).  As such, the example, 18073A3 is test A3 conducted 
on the 73rd day of 2018.  But the test could also be early on the next (e.g., 74th) day because 
the day portion of ACME test numbers is not ch anged when operations continue past 
midnight (whether GMT or Eastern) into the start of the next day. 
 
Tests numbers that only differ by the final digit (e.g., 18073A1, 18073A2, and 18073A3) are 
normally similar and occasionally identical, where the number  is indicative of the variant or 
repeat.  Letters I and O are not used because of their similarity to 1 and 0.  While initially used 
in ACME testing, the letter E ceased to be used because Excel software (for example) by 
default interpreted it as represent ing an exponential which was inconvenient.  As a related 
aside, ACME tests are often not conducted in order and some planned tests are regularly not 
conducted at all.  The latter results in additional letter gaps if the tests from a day are sorted 
alphabetically.  However, ACME testing often begins with test A1, where the final digit begins 
with 1 and is increased from there with variant or repeat tests for the same letter. 
 
The basic data is in a CSV format, i.e., with comma-separated values, which can be opened by 
spreadsheet software such as Microsoft Excel.  The first row is a header and provides a limited 
summary of the hardware and operational conditions.  The header is explained in Section 10.1 
Metadata Header of ACME-DOC-010 Rev D, which begins on page 126.

Additional conversion is required for the measurements of the gas flow rates found in second 
through fourth columns of the data file.  By column (e.g., in Excel), the flow rates are for: 
 B MFCA1StaticMixerFlow  oxidizer (or inert) gas 
 C MFCA2BypassFlow  nitrogen 
 D MFCA3FuelFlow  fuel 
 
The ACME MFCs are Hastings HFC -D-302 with default standard temperature and pressures 
(STP) of 0 Celsius and 760 torr for computations of volume tric flow or velocity.  It is very 
important to understand that slpm, i.e., standard liters per minute, is a measurement of the 
mass (or alternatively molar) flow rate wh ich is independent of the actual temperature or 
pressure.  This is discussed for examp le in the MFC manual, where chapter 9 (on page 39) is 
“Volumetric vs. Mass Flow.” 
 
Conversions for the actual gases can be made with information provided in the manual on 
subsequent pages and the spreadsheets GasDataTables.xls and Inside gas mix calculator .xls 
which were both provided by the MFC manufacturer.  First, open GasDataTable s.xls and 
enable content, and then open Inside gas mix calculator.xls and enable content.  From this, 
one should be able to enter mixtures and compute appropriate Gas Conversio n Factors 
(GCFs). 
 
Alternatively, the conversions above can be made with spreadsheets prepared by the ACME 
engineering team, where options include the following: 
ACMEFlowCalculator.xlsx 
ACMEFlowAndPressuresRawDataConversion.xlsx 
  
Computation of gas veloci ties, for example for the gas -jet and coflow burners, depends on 
burner dimensions.  For the coflow burner, ACME mechanical design engineer Adrian Drake 
indicated the following: 
 
“The inner diameter of the inner tube is .085 inches.   Its cross sectional  area is .00567 
in^2, or 3.66 x 10^-6 m^2.  
  
The flow of the outer tube is a little tricky to calculate, based on where and how you 
measure.  There’s an internal step in the outer tube that can’t be seen from outside, and 
blocks the flow to the outer honeycombs.  Because of this, when we calculated the cross-
sectional are of the outer tube, it measures from the inner step.   
  
The outer tube’s outer diameter at that step is 0.984 inches.  Its cross section area at that 
diameter is .7605 in^2.   From this we  have to subtract the inner tube’s outer 
diameter:  .095 inch diameter, so area is .00709.   Subtract from the Outer Diameter and 
we get .7534 in^2, or .000486 m^2.”

4. Space Acceleration Measurement System (SAMS) data 
The Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR) in which the experiment was conducted, was equipped 
with a Passive Rack Isolation System (PaRIS), but it was not utilized for ACME testing because 
of the need for crew time to enable and disable the isolation (by respectively removing and 
installing alignment guides) on each test day.  However, the CIR was equipped with a 3 -axis 
SAMS sensor head.  The acceleration archives, along with relevant documentation, are 
accessible from https://gipoc.grc.nasa.gov/wp/pims/home/, the home page for the Principal 
Investigator Microgravity Services (PIMS). 
 
The following guidance for the SAMS data was provided by Kenneth Hrovat (NASA Glenn):  In 
addition to the documentation and links from the PIMS home page, we introduce a few ways 
below to get started with exploring the CIR microgravity environment taking the perspective 
of SAMS measurements from within the rack. 
 
Roadmap Spectrograms 
To get a high-level, spectral roadmap view within two days after measurement, you can: 
a. Use your browser and start at this link: http://pims.grc.nasa.gov/roadmap

b. In the upper left pane, you can navigate by GMT date to see a list of plots (PDF files) 
in the right pane for the GMT date of your choice.  We refer to these PDFs as roadmap 
plots.  For SAMS, these are acceleration spectrograms, which for vibratory data are a 
good way to look at patterns, structures and boundaries in both time and frequency 
for long time spans -- nominally, 8 hours per plot.  These spectrograms are mostly 
qualitative and crudely quantitative three -dimensional plots in that a color scale 
attempts to map power spectral density magnitude as a color for the 3rd dimension. 
NOTE: Ticking the Navigation Filter for “es05” in the bottom pane will then only 
highlight days in yellow in the upper left pane when the operations team powered on 
the SAMS sensor in the CIR to take measurements. 
c. You might want to navigate directly to a GMT day of interest for your science 
operations or run, but you can also browse day -by-day too in order to get some 
context comparing vario us 8 -hour spans to get a feel for the environment under 
various conditions.

2. ACME Data Camera (DataCam) 
ACME’s prim ary camera  is a high-definition (1360 x 1024), 12 -bit color camera mounted 
outside the chamber on CIR’s optical bench.  It is equipped with a motorized lens with 
independent control of the zoom, iris, and focus.  It has a filter barrel with a fused silica 
window (for color imaging), a blue -green BG7 Schott glass filter, and bandpass filters for 
imaging CH* and HCHO (formaldehyde) chemiluminescence.

and Thin Filament Pyrometry (TFP), soot volume fraction, and quantitative determination of 
the CH* concentration.

(Yale U.), where Python is an open source programming language that is easily obtainable for

In order to use the ACME DataCam for quantitative measurements it is critical to avoid 
saturation, for example by using a camera gain of zero.  Another employed approach was to 
not use the camera’s white balance which by default multiplies the red and blue by 1.41 and 
1.68, respectively, because that could inadvertently lead to saturation.  Such multiplication 
can be done after the HOBJ file is converted and the colors demosaiced, where in OMA2 the 
white balancing is accomplished with the commands: 
*rgb 1.41 1 1.68 
clip 4095 
In summary, the white balance, if desired, needs to be done after the fact through software 
because it was generally not done with the hardware to avoid the risk of saturation.  
 
Note that the ACME images will be naturally dark because of the use of the 16-bit file format 
with 12 -bit image data.  As such,  adjustments to the imag e brightness will generally be 
necessary even if the images are not demos aiced.  Furthermore, cameras’ CCD arrays are 
susceptible to radiation damage while in orbit.  As a result, the images feature hot pixels which 
increase in number over the months and years in space.  As such, it will appear as if there is a 
star field within the combustion chamber.  Therefore, the images should ideally be processed 
to remove the bright outlying pixels.  The radiation damage is also obvious in the ops camera 
imaging. 
 
Although the DataCam was typically set for imaging at 30 fps, that is trumped by the exposure 
settings which were often long because of the dim nature of the microgravity flames.  As a 
result, the framing rate was typically far less.  Different exposures could have been selected 
for different phases of the tests, e.g., prior to ignition and during the test, so the framing rate 
may not be consistent .  Bracketed imaging could also be employed, where a repeating 
sequence of different exposure  durations is used to enable appropriate photography under 
uncertain or varying flame intensities.  Bracketing is also used to simultaneously accomplish 
multiple imaging objectives, e.g., to appropriately image both brightly glowing soot (or TFP 
fibers) as well as the much dimmer blue portion of the flame.

Due to the use of a turning mirror, the DataCam images should be flipped vertically if upright 
images are desired, i.e., where the burner is at the bottom and the flame extends upward.  In 
this regard, note that the DataCam provides images in a ‘landscape’ format with the image’s 
long dimension orth ogonal to the burner and chamber axis.  This is contrary to a n ACME 
science requirement specifying that the long dimension be aligned with the experimental axis 
to provide images in a ‘portrait’ format.   In this regard, the science requirements were not 
always met, e.g., because of design tradeoffs, etc.

scaling can typically be carried out using images regularly taken of the ceramic fibers for Thin

4. High Bit-depth Multi-spectral (HiBMs) camera and CIR illumination package 
The HiBMs camera was used in combination with an opposed backlight, i.e., the CIR ’s 
collimated illumination package.   The resultant imaging was used in preceding droplet 
combustion experiments for measuring droplet diameter , but it may also be useful for 
determining the soot volume fraction via light extinction.  However, the camera’s red filter , 
corresponding to the red illumination, is broad enough to permit significant soot 
incandescence to pass through the filter.  As a result, this imaging is unlikely to be useful for 
soot volume fraction measurements for all conditions.  After post-processing on the ground, 
the images were made available in the TIFF format  and as such are readily op ened.  The 
grayscale images were typically archived at 15 fps.

difficult to avoi d optical interference in all circumstances, where design trade -offs were 
necessary.  However, the soot volume fraction can also be computed using images from the 
ACME DataCam as discussed in that camera’s section.  
 
In initial ACME testing with the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment, the 
monochrome camera was used without a filter, but that was abandoned because the camera 
is nearly insensitive to blue.  The red filter was used throughout E-FIELD Flames testing. 
 
 
C. Test Parameters and Other Files 
Files discussed in this section include the following: 
Test parameter file 
Notes 
Test timing 
Logs 
 
1. Test parameter file 
Each E-FIELD Flames test was controlled by a software script and corresponding parameter 
file, named for example 18073A3_VoltageSweep.txt, specifying settings for imaging, ignition, 
gas flow, and more.    The parameter file and script are discussed extensively in the ACME/E-
FIELD Flames Experimental Procedure document, which should be referred to for information 
on this file type.  But note that the ACME.log files should be the best way to verify the as-run 
test parameters, where the separate parameter file s are unfortunately not completely 
reliable.  They were not always saved and as a result were sometimes recreated after the fact. 
 
2. Notes 
Notes of various types are available for test days, where not all types were collected or 
necessarily prepared for each test day.  It is important to understand that the notes, whether 
handwritten or typed, may include mistakes.  It is better to rely on automated logs, metadata 
files, and parameter files or of course the measurements themselves when possible. 
 
The daily test point logs, with names such as ‘18087 daily test point log.pdf,’ chronologically 
list the tests and the image data acquired which was to be downlinked.  Typically, image data 
was not downlinked without successful, i.e., sustained, ignition.  As an e xample, image data 
wasn’t downlinked if the t est aborted prior to ignition.  However, the reverse is not always 
true.  Image data was sometimes downlinked when ignition was unsuccessful and as a result 
the test point log is only a clue as to what transpired.  Furthermore, note that image data 
from certain selected cameras , typically the HiBMs and/or C FI intensified camera (s), was 
sometimes not downlinked due to the limit of how much data could be reasonabl y 
downlinked in a subsequent two-shift day. 
 
Notes with names such as ‘18087 efield & velocities traces.pdf’ include preliminary plots of 
the electrode mesh voltage and flow rates (as seen on a console during testing), typically along 
with additional information about the tests.

➔ Agree to terms 
➔ Add shortcuts

by gaseous fuels: L ,       , hc , and LSP .

Heat flux measurement 
• The incident heat flux is conductive and radiative. 
• Heat flux gages in the surface will record incident 
heat flux. 
• Thermocouples in the surface will record the Ts 
distribution. 
• Surface reradiative flux scales with Ts4. 
• Flow rate will be varied to obtain a wide range of L.

--- page 6 ---
6 
Motivation 
• Many condensed fuels have a critical heat flux (CHF) 
below which ignition and spread is impossible.  
 
• This CHF depends on the surface heat loss rate. 
 
• Buoyant cooling occurs in 1g, so CHF is lower in g. 
 
• BRE will identify g CHF for condensed fuels. 
 
Condensed fuel ignition in g will generally be easier 
than on Earth.

NASA Test 1 
• The Upward Flame Propagation 
(Test 1) is NASA’s main test for 
material flammability in 
spacecraft. 
• It tests the flame propagation of a 
vertical sample 30  6.4 – 7.5 cm.  
• Burn length (15 cm threshold) and 
time are recorded.  
• It is a pass-fail test. 
• It is similar to UL-94, whose 
performance scales with hc / L .

--- page 8 ---
8 
NASA Test 2 
• Ohlemiller (1992) found that NASA Test 1 results did not 
correlate with NIST flammability tests of ignition, burning 
rate, and opposed flow flame spread. 
• He recommended that Test 1 be modified to include the 
addition of external radiant heating. 
• NASA Test 2 uses a cone calorimeter with heat fluxes of 
25 – 75 kW/m2 to supplement Test 1. 
• Tests 1 and 2 do not explicitly predict performance in g. 
Ohlemiller recommended g tests, 
stating “Without such information 
spacecraft designers are confronted 
with unquantifiable risks regarding 
fire safety.”

Relating material properties to g flammability 
Can 1g properties be related to g flammability? 
 
• This works for to UL-94 in 1g, whose performance 
scales with hc / L . 
• If it works in g, a limited number of g tests could 
be used to predict ignition, burning, and extinction  
behavior of many materials. 
 
A BRE goal is to furnish g measurements relating 
ignition, burning, and extinction to material properties 
that are obtainable in 1g.

g combustion surprises 
• Quasisteady burning of condensed fuels in g has 
rarely been observed. 
• Several surprises were seen in tests of g droplets 
and flame spread, including those associated with 
3D effects, flaming fire brands, radiative extinction, 
etc. 
• The effects of radiative feedback have not been 
explored.

The MIR fire 
• In the MIR fire a solid propellant oxygen generator 
ignited, producing a 1 m flame. 
• If this flame had impinged on other materials, could 
they have burned self-sustained? 
• Material flammability in space must be better 
understood.

Past condensed fuel combustion in g 
• Experiments in Skylab indicated sustained burning 
of thin paper and plastics. 
• Spread on 4.5 mm diameter rods of Delrin, PMMA 
and HDPE was achieved in opposed flows down to 
1 cm/s. 
• Opposed flow spread in quiescent g depends on 
material, thickness, and oxygen concentration. 
• Droplet flames of Kumagai, Williams, and co-
workers.

Candles in g 
• Candle flames on MIR survived from 1 – 45 
min. Hemispherical flames were observed. 
• Dietrich and T’ien’s glovebox tests had 
much shorter durations. 
• The MIR flights had XO2 = 0.22 – 0.25 and a 
metal screen enclosure. 
• The glovebox had XO2 = 0.217 and a 
perforated plastic enclosure. 
Sustained burning of condensed fuels in g without 
external radiation is confirmed.

• Planar surfaces in g can produce spherical flames.

uniform flow.

Experimental Objectives 
1. Observe burning behavior in long-duration g for 
CH4/N2 and C2H4/N2 mixtures on 25 and 50 mm

4. Consider the conditions of Objectives 1 – 2 for

Summary of approach for ISS tests 
• Burn tests. The chamber contents will be set. Fuel flow 
will commence followed by ignition. The flames will be 
observed without varying the fuel or its flow rate. Ts and 
L will vary, allowing diverse conditions to be observed. 
 
• Ignition tests. The chamber contents will be set. Fuel 
flow will commence followed by attempted ignition. 
 
• Extinction limit tests. The chamber contents will be set. 
Flames will be ignited followed by a gradual reduction in 
fuel flow rate until extinction.

Justification for extended-duration g 
• NASA Tests 1 and 2 are inadequate for analyzing 
material fire hazards in spacecraft. 
• Past and planned g tests of condensed fuels are 
insufficient to cover the diverse condensed fuels 
used in spacecraft. 
• The BRE tests cannot be performed in parabolic 
aircraft owing to g-jitter. Drop tower times are too 
short compared to the characteristic times for the 
gas phase (~20 s) and Ts (~40 s).  
• Only long-duration g tests will allow the 
establishment of g flammability maps.

--- page 58 ---

Requirements discussion 
BRE Burners 
• The burners shall involve round porous frits of 25 
and 50 mm diameters.  
• Each burner shall contain two heat flux meters flush 
with the frit surface and facing outward.  
• The heat flux meters shall be maintained at 65 – 85 
°C to avoid condensation. 
• The burners shall be positioned such that the burner 
axis is coincident with the CIR chamber axis and 
orthogonal to the CIR chamber window axes. 
• The plane of the CIR chamber window centers shall 
be 10±2 mm downstream of the frit surface. 
 
ISRD Section 1.1: Accepted 
ISRD Sections 1.2-1.7: Not Applicable

--- page 59 ---
59 
Requirements discussion 
Burner Gas Delivery 
Required flow rates for BRE are: 
• Fuel: 0 – 0.1 slpm (N2 basis) 
• Oxidizer: Not applicable 
• Inert: 0 – 2 slpm (N2 basis) 
 
These requirements were established by 
observations of flames in 1g, in the 5 s drop 
facility, and on the parabolic aircraft. 
 
ISRD Section 1.8: Accepted. 
ISRD Section 1.9: Accepted for the above flows.

--- page 60 ---
60 
Requirements discussion 
Ignition 
• Ignition disturbances should be minimized 
to allow undisturbed burning quickly. 
Ignition should occur ~ 3 mm from the frit 
surface.  
• BRE is unique among ACME experiments 
in its interest in ignition limits. Thus the 
igniter conditions must be highly repeatable. 
 
ISRD Section 1.10: Accepted with the above 
qualifications.

--- page 61 ---
61 
Requirements discussion 
Ambient Environment 
• Prior to each test, the chamber shall contain the 
specified gas composition within ±0.01 mole fraction. A 
cylindrical free area that is (1) at least 200 mm in 
diameter, (2) at least 100 mm downstream of the frit 
surface, and (3) at least 50 mm upstream, shall be kept 
clear of solid objects. The temporary insertion of the 
igniter and probes are allowed exceptions. 
 
• Suggested methods to decrease bottled gas 
consumption rates include, as available, partial venting 
and filling, and scrubbing for CO2 and H2O. 
 
ISRD Section 1.11: Accepted

ISRD Section 1.12: Accepted with the above

Operational sequence for ignition,  
burn, and extinction tests 
 Action Time 
1. Install burner and flush the plumbing. 
2. Establish chamber conditions (p and species). 
3. Allow 5 mins for chamber contents to reach equilibrium. 
4. Take reference images. Begin color imaging. Begin 
monitoring measurements and wait 15 s. 
5. Ignition test: Commence fluent flow, energize igniter, and 
retract igniter. If flame ignites, proceed to Step 6 or Step 
7. Otherwise proceed to Step 8. 
6. Burn test. Allow the flame to burn until extinction or 
quasisteady. Detect extinction with a PMT or with near 
real-time video. Reduce flow rate to confirm extinction. 
7. Extinction test. Burn for 20 s then reduce flow rate 
gradually until extinction. 
8. Record reference images. After 60 s terminate color 
imaging and monitoring measurements. Select data for 
downlink and downlink data.

Post flight data analysis plan 
• Much post-flight data analysis will occur during mission 
ops. Analysis will include: plots of Ts, incident flame heat 
flux, and L versus time; and analysis of other discrete 
signals for evidence of quasi-steadiness. 
 
• Extensive image analysis will be performed and, where 
possible, compared with g images of condensed fuel 
flames. 
 
• We will map out the flammable conditions and burning 
rates of diverse condensed fuels. Flammability maps for 
each ambient condition will be prepared on 3-axis plots, 
where the axes are hc, L, and reradiation heat flux.
